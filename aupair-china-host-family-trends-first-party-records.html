Who Hosts an Au Pair in China?

Current first-party family profiles point to a relationship centred on one child, natural language exposure, cultural exchange, and enough time to build a stable routine.

51non-test public family profiles, 2025–2026
32 of 50focus mainly on one child
35prefer six months or longer

1. Most profiles describe companionship

Forty-four of 51 current profiles mention childcare or companionship. Among the 50 profiles with a stated care scope, 32 focus mainly on one child. The common picture is not a large household looking for a general worker; it is a family trying to add a trusted person to one child's daily life.

2. Language exposure is part of family life

Thirty-two profiles mention English or another language, while only seven foreground teaching or tutoring. For an aupair China match, that difference matters: natural language exposure can happen through meals, games, school runs, and conversation without turning the au pair into a private teacher.

3. Cultural exchange and fit remain central

Twenty-eight profiles mention cultural exchange, and 28 mention personality, compatibility, or long-term fit. Families are not only asking whether someone can help. They are asking whether the person can communicate, adapt, and become a steady part of the child's routine.

4. Stability matters more than a short visit

Thirty-five current profiles prefer six months or longer. A longer stay can support continuity, but only when both sides agree on weekly hours, private space, days off, pocket money, expenses, and the support available if expectations drift.

5. Boundary flags require conversation

Seventeen profiles trigger an automatic household-work or nanny-boundary flag. A flag is not an accusation. It identifies where wording about tasks, hours, or household help should prompt a direct conversation before anyone commits to a match.

Method and privacy boundary. The analysis keeps four evidence layers separate: 450 enquiry and call-log leads from 2015; six verified arrivals or placements from 2017; 51 current non-test public family profiles from 2025–2026; and 14 local family packs used qualitatively only. The findings describe one programme and are not official national statistics. No names, contact details, identity documents, precise addresses, or identifiable child information are published.
