English · 中文版 · All guides

When a family starts looking for an au pair, it is tempting to begin with nationality or language and imagine the children chatting over dinner with an older brother or sister from abroad. Sharing a home brings earlier questions: who agrees to host, which room is available, who is responsible after school, and how weekends will work.

Concrete answers make it easier to get to know a candidate. A discussion at home can also show you which arrangements still need work. There is no pass mark for this checklist, and it cannot replace meeting and checking details with the person you may host.

Do the people sharing the home agree?

Talk with your partner and any relatives who live with you. One person may want more English around the children; another may worry about privacy; someone else may assume there will be a person available to cover any gap. Discuss those expectations together. Decide who handles day-to-day communication and disagreements before presenting the family to a candidate.

If one adult travels frequently and another already handles most household arrangements, ask the person doing that work whether they want to host. Shopping, explaining routines and helping with unfamiliar errands take time. Work out who is willing to do those things before choosing a candidate.

Is the room ready, and is everyday support available?

Prepare a private room that the au pair can use throughout the stay. Discuss bathroom access, meals and storage for personal belongings. If the room still holds things the whole family collects every day, resolve that first. A candidate will also want to know what is nearby and whom they can ask when they need help with something unfamiliar. [1]

Who is responsible for the children's care?

Walk through a school day. Who collects the child, who takes over after a shared activity, and what happens when a parent works late? Parents need a reliable plan for their child's care and safety, then they can discuss the activities a candidate is comfortable joining. If you need specialist care or someone to cover every hour, arrange an appropriate service separately.

Time off should be understandable, too. You can invite someone to a family dinner, a weekend outing or an occasional change of plan. Living in the house does not mean always being available. Agree how you will discuss changes.

Is an adult willing to keep the conversation going?

A family that is not fluent in English can use another shared language and translation tools. An adult still needs to take responsibility for ongoing communication about dates, travel and household needs, and be willing to ask again when something is unclear. Children should not have to coordinate arrangements between adults.

Try a small situation in the first call. If Wednesday's activity is cancelled, how much notice would the au pair receive? If your child no longer wants to play, whom should they contact? Working through those questions tells both sides more than saying that everyone is easy to get along with.

Agree how to raise an awkward issue. Someone might prefer to send a message first, then talk when the child is elsewhere. You can check in before anyone becomes upset. After a discussion, confirm that both people understand the same arrangement and write down a change if that would help.

Do the budget and dates fit the family's plans?

List accommodation and meals, agreed pocket money, everyday travel and any services you may choose, and decide who pays for what. Check arrangements for study, travel, insurance and coming to China individually. If a move, renovation or a long work trip is approaching, tell the candidate and look at the dates together.

Can both sides have the stay they are expecting?

A family may want its child to hear another language. A candidate may want to study Chinese, explore the city and get to know ordinary life in China. Leave time for their questions and ask how they hope to spend days off. Expectations of professional teaching, round-the-clock care and unplanned extra hours may not fit someone looking for cultural exchange, even when their language skills seem suitable.

Once the household agrees and the practical arrangements are clear, you can begin getting to know specific candidates. If something still needs a decision, deal with it first. Creating a profile does not oblige you to rush into a match.

什么样的家庭适合接待互惠生

准备接待互惠生时，家庭可能先去挑国籍、看语言，再想象孩子和外国大哥哥、大姐姐一起吃饭聊天。真要住在一起，还有一些更早要谈的事：家里谁同意接待，房间在哪里，孩子放学以后谁负责，周末又怎样安排。

这些问题能谈出具体答案，才方便与候选人继续了解。家庭自查也可以帮你发现暂时没准备好的部分。它没有统一分数，不能代替双方见面核实。

一起住的人是否同意接待

先和伴侣、同住长辈说说自己的期待。有人希望孩子多说英语，有人担心家里不方便，也有人以为来了一位能随时顶班的人。把这些想法放在同一次讨论里，定下日常由谁沟通、意见不同时由谁协调，再向候选人介绍家庭。

如果家里一位成员经常出差，另一位承担了大部分日常安排，要先听承担者是否愿意接待。采购、介绍生活规则、陪同处理琐事都需要时间。这些事若没人愿意负责，先补上安排，比先挑选人选更有用。

房间和生活支持是否已经落实

准备好可以稳定使用的独立房间，讨论洗浴、吃饭和个人物品怎样安排。房间如果还堆着全家每天都要取用的东西，就先解决这些实际问题。候选人还需要知道附近生活是否方便，以及遇到不会处理的事情可以找谁。[1]

孩子的照顾安排是否有人负责

拿一个上学日来试着安排：放学谁接，活动结束后谁接手，家长加班时有没有其他安排。接待互惠生前，父母仍需要落实孩子的照顾和安全责任，再与候选人确认适合参与的事情。若目标是专业照护或全天候替班，应另外寻找相应服务。

休息时间也要能说清楚。家庭聚餐、周末出游或临时帮忙，可以提前邀请和商量，不能默认住在家里就一直有空。安排改变时，双方需要有重新确认的方法。

家庭有没有稳定的沟通人

家里英语不够流利，可以使用双方能理解的共同语言和翻译工具，但总要有一位成年人愿意持续沟通。聊天之外，还要能把日期、出行和生活要求说清楚，遇到误会愿意再问一遍。不要让孩子承担成年人之间的协调。

第一次通话可以试着谈一件小事：如果周三的活动要取消，会提前多久告诉对方？如果孩子不愿继续玩，该联系谁？双方能把这些问题聊明白，比只说“我们都很好相处”更能帮助判断。

也约好怎样提出不方便说的事。比如先写一条消息，再选一个孩子不在场的时间谈。家庭不需要等到有人生气才沟通。说完以后，确认双方理解的是同一件事，必要时把新的安排简单记下来。

预算和接待时间是否安排得过来

把食宿、约定的零用钱、日常出行和可能选择的服务费用列出来，说明哪些由谁承担。学校、旅行、保险及来华所需安排，应分别按实际情况核实。家里近期如果搬家、装修或长时间出差，也要告诉候选人，再一起看接待日期是否合适。

双方期待能不能在同一种生活里实现

家庭希望孩子接触外语，候选人也可能想学习中文、了解城市和中国人的日常生活。通话时给对方提问的时间，问问她或他想怎样度过休息日。若家庭期待专业外教、全天候照护和随时加班，而对方想参加文化交流，即使语言条件合适，生活安排也可能谈不拢。

家庭已经形成共同意见、生活安排也基本清楚，就可以开始了解具体人选。还有一项谈不妥，也可以先处理它，不必因为资料已经注册就急着决定。
