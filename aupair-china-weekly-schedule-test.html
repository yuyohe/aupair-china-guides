Aupair China: The Weekly Schedule Test Before You Match

A one-page conversation guide for au pairs and Chinese host families

A host family can be warm, honest and enthusiastic, yet still describe the au pair's week as 'a few hours a day, flexible.' That is not quite a plan. The important questions often appear after the first call: Is the afternoon really free? Who takes over when a parent is late? Does 'help with the children' include cooking for the whole household? A simple written week can prevent both sides from having to guess.

Start with a real Tuesday, not a weekly total

Ask the family to walk through a normal school day from breakfast to bedtime. Write down the times when the au pair is responsible for a child, the times another adult takes over, and the times the au pair is genuinely free to leave, study or meet friends. Repeat the exercise for one weekend day. A number such as 'five hours' tells you less than a timetable that shows where those hours sit.

This is especially useful when the day is split into a morning block and an evening block. The hours between them may look free on paper, but the arrangement feels different if the au pair must stay at home in case plans change. Name that difference before either side commits.

Separate free time from standby time

Ask plainly: 'If the child is at school from 9 to 3, may I leave the house, attend a language class and make my own plans?' If the answer is 'usually, unless we need you,' discuss how often the exception happens, how much notice is given, and what happens when a class has already been paid for.

A family does not have to know every future disruption. It does need a fair way to handle changes. A fixed weekly check-in and a shared calendar are more useful than a vague promise to be flexible.

Put each duty beside its time block

'Help with the children' can mean reading stories, school pickup, snacks, English conversation, bedtime routines or something else. Write the actual activities beside the relevant hours. Mark which adult is present, who makes decisions about the child, and whether the au pair would ever be the only adult responsible.

Also write down what is outside the role. Child-related tidying is not the same as open-ended cleaning, cooking or caring for other relatives. If a task matters to the family, it should be discussed directly rather than hidden inside the words 'part of the family.'

Ask who takes over when plans change

A realistic schedule needs a handoff. What happens if a parent gets home late, the child is sick, school closes, or a grandparent cannot help that day? Who calls whom, and by what time? Is the change optional or expected? Is there an agreed way to adjust the rest of the week?

These questions are not a sign of distrust. They let the family see where it needs its own backup plan, and they protect the au pair from quietly becoming the backup for every emergency.

Protect study, privacy and a life outside the home

Many applicants choose an aupair China stay partly for language learning and cultural exchange. A workable plan therefore includes the au pair's own study time, rest, privacy and days away from the home. Ask which blocks are predictable enough for a course and whether family invitations are invitations, not obligations during free time.

The family should also explain the private living space and ordinary household routines. A timetable is more credible when the person living it has somewhere to rest and permission to make independent plans.

Use this one-page weekly schedule test

Before confirming a match, fill in these seven lines together: (1) the childcare blocks for each day; (2) the named child-related activities in each block; (3) the adult responsible before and after each block; (4) genuinely free blocks, including whether the au pair may leave; (5) fixed study time and full days off; (6) how changes are requested, recorded and balanced; and (7) the person to contact if the plan stops matching reality.

Leave a blank for questions that neither side can answer yet. A blank is better than a made-up promise. After the video call, share a short written recap and invite corrections. The goal is not to create a rigid minute-by-minute contract; it is to make the ordinary week understandable to both people.

What a promising answer sounds like

Look for specificity and willingness to revise: 'On school days we need help before school and again from 4 to 7. Your afternoons are your own. We will ask at least a day ahead if a change is needed, and we will agree on a swap before assuming you are available.' That example is illustrative, not a standard schedule or a claim about any particular family.

Slow down if the answers keep changing, free time depends on being constantly reachable, or no adult will take responsibility when plans shift. It is fair for either side to decide that a friendly family is still not the right match.

A message you can send after the call

'Thank you for speaking with me. I enjoyed learning about your family. Before we decide, could we put a normal school day and one weekend day into a short shared schedule? I would like to confirm the childcare blocks, who takes over afterwards, which time is fully free, my study time, and what we would do when plans change. I can send my understanding first so you can correct anything I missed.'

A match does not become cold because the practical details are written down. Clarity gives warmth a place to last.
