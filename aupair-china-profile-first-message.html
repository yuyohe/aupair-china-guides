Aupair China: Write a profile someone can reply to

By Au Pair Ren

A family opens an au pair profile and reads, "I love children and want to experience Chinese culture." The introduction sounds friendly. They still have to work out what to ask next.

An applicant has the same problem with a family profile that says, "We are a warm family looking for someone responsible." What does an afternoon in that home look like? When would the au pair have time for Mandarin lessons?

If you are starting an Aupair China search, give the person reading your profile a few details they can respond to. Au Pair Ren is a China-based matching platform where Chinese host families and overseas applicants can read profiles and begin a conversation through the website.

Describe something you have actually done

Applicants can start with their experience around children. Say who you spent time with, roughly how old the children were, and what you did together. Reading a picture book with a younger cousin is different from supervising a group at a summer camp. Both give a family something to ask about.

For example, an applicant with that experience could write: "I looked after my seven-year-old cousin on Saturday afternoons. We made lunch with an adult nearby, read together and went to the park. I would like to keep studying Mandarin while living with a family in China." Use your own experience and leave out anything that does not describe you.

Mention your language level in ordinary terms. If you can follow everyday English but need time to answer, say that. A first video conversation is easier when both sides know what to expect.

Families can describe a normal weekday

A city name and a family photo leave plenty unexplained. Write about the part of the day an au pair would share with you. Include the children's age ranges and the activities you have in mind.

A family might write: "Our daughter comes home from school in the late afternoon. We would like someone who enjoys reading and playing with her in English. We can discuss which afternoons would fit around your Mandarin classes." This is an example of wording, not a published family listing.

If you have a room available, describe it. Say how you would discuss a weekly schedule and time off. Put the arrangement you can offer into words before asking someone to travel.

Write about your interests without trying to fit every family

Cooking, football, drawing or a quiet evening with a book can all belong in a profile. Add one detail: what do you cook, which sport do you play, or what would you enjoy doing with a child? A family with a similar interest has a natural place to begin.

Your preferred arrival period and length of stay also help. Keep them current. If your plans change, update the profile so that a family does not spend its first message asking about a date that has already passed.

Use the profile to write the first message

Read the other person's introduction before sending a message. Pick something specific and ask a question you would genuinely like answered.

An applicant could ask: "I saw that your family enjoys cooking. Would there be time for me to join you in the kitchen on weekends? I also want to take Mandarin classes. What does a usual weekday look like?" A family could ask an applicant to tell them more about an activity mentioned in their profile.

Keep the first exchange on the platform while you get to know each other. You can discuss private contact details when you are ready.

Start with a profile you can stand behind

On Au Pair Ren, browse the public introductions and notice which ones make you want to ask a question. Then look at your own profile. Could someone tell why you want to take part and what you would like everyday life to be like?

Add the missing detail before sending your next message. A short, specific introduction gives the next person something to reply to.
