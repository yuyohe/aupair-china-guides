Aupair China: 7 Icebreakers for an Awkward First Week

The front door closes. Everyone smiles. The new au pair has arrived, the children are curious, and the host parents want the first evening to feel welcoming. Then the room goes quiet.

That silence is not evidence of a bad match. A first week is full of invisible questions. The au pair is learning a new home and rhythm. The children are deciding what this unfamiliar adult means for their routine. The parents want connection, but do not want to force it.

In an aupair China placement, the best first-week activities are usually short, concrete and easy to stop. They give everyone something to do together, so conversation does not have to carry the whole relationship.

These ideas are adapted from AuPair Ren's practitioner experience with more than 500 au pairs and host families. They are practical prompts, not a controlled study or a guarantee. Children participate voluntarily; parents remain responsible for supervision, privacy, allergies and household safety.

01. Put two pins on a world map

Place one pin where the au pair grew up and another where the host family lives. Each person gets three minutes to introduce one place: the weather, a food, a local expression or somewhere they would take a visitor. Younger children can use pictures; older children can plan a pretend route between the two places.

Why it helps: nobody has to invent a topic, and both sides get to be the expert.

02. Become a tour guide in your own city

Ask the child and the au pair to choose three local places: one famous, one useful and one personal favorite. Mark them on a map and build a tiny Saturday itinerary. If the family later takes the walk, the child can lead one section while the au pair asks questions.

Keep the route private. Do not share the home address, school details or children's live location online.

03. Try a hometown dish show-and-tell

Each person introduces one food connected to home. Start with a picture, smell, memory or story. If everyone is interested, choose one simple recipe to prepare together. Decorating biscuits, assembling fruit or folding dumplings can be easier than attempting a complicated first-week dinner.

Confirm allergies and kitchen rules with the parents before shopping or cooking.

04. Build a shared festival calendar

Let each person add two celebrations that matter to them. Use four prompts: When is it? Who joins? What do people do? What does it mean to you? The last question turns a list of customs into personal storytelling and avoids treating any country as one uniform culture.

05. Record a two-minute family newsroom

One person presents, one gives the weather, and another acts out a harmless story from the day: “A missing sock was found under the sofa,” or “Tomorrow's forecast is a 90 percent chance of noodles.”

Do not post the recording by default. The camera can be a prop, or everyone can watch and delete the clip together. A cardboard television frame works for children who dislike being filmed.

06. Play bilingual Pictionary

Begin with objects everyone can draw badly: bicycle, cat, dumpling, umbrella. The person who guesses says the word in one language; another player offers it in the second. Nobody loses a point for pronunciation. Later, try gentle abstract words such as “welcome” or “teamwork” and ask why each drawing represented the word.

07. Turn stereotypes into questions

Write a few harmless assumptions on cards: “Everyone in this country eats the same breakfast,” or “All children like the same games.” Decide whether each is a fact, a guess or a question that needs checking. Then replace it with something specific: “What did breakfast look like in your home?”

Avoid accents, costumes or imitations of a nationality. The goal is to notice shortcuts in our thinking and become more curious—not to perform a culture.

A simple first-week rhythm

  1. Explain the activity in one sentence.
  2. Let the child choose whether to join.
  3. Give everyone a small role.
  4. Stop while the energy is still good.
  5. Ask whether to repeat, change or replace it next time.

One 15- to 25-minute activity every other day is enough.

What success actually looks like

The first win may be modest: the child asks one spontaneous question, the au pair learns the name of a neighborhood park, or everyone laughs at the same terrible drawing. Those moments matter because they create the next conversation.

A good aupair China first week does not need to look impressive on social media. It needs to feel safe enough for people to be curious, make mistakes and try again.
