Aupair China: When Grandparents Live With Your Host Family

By Au Pair Ren

Try this question on a host-family video call: a parent has planned English picture books after school, but a grandparent thinks the child needs a rest. Who should you check with before changing the plan?

It is a small situation worth discussing before you match. When grandparents live with a host family, you will share everyday life with them too. In an Aupair China search, ask who lives at home and how the adults agree on the child's routine.

Ask who will actually be at home

A profile saying "grandparents are nearby" leaves a few questions open. Do they live in the same apartment, visit during the week or stay for part of the year? Who usually shares meals with the family?

Ask what you would do together. A grandparent who knows the child's habits can show you a favourite book or explain a familiar after-school routine. Find out which parts of that routine you would join and which an adult family member would continue to handle.

Agree on a parent to contact when plans change

Ask the host parents to name the person who will confirm your weekly arrangements. If another adult suggests a different plan, you need to know whom to contact. The parents can discuss the change within the family and give you one clear answer.

You could ask: "If you and a grandparent suggest different activities, which parent should I check with? How should I reach you while you are out?" Agree on this while everyone has time to talk. Keep the child supervised while you check an unclear arrangement.

Meet the grandparents before you arrive

If they will share the home, invite them to join part of a video call. Introduce yourself and ask which language they feel comfortable using. Tell them about your own Mandarin level. Language preferences are individual; ask each person.

A parent can help with translation. For practical details, follow the call with a short written message that both sides have checked. Names, times and a plan for the next day are easier to look up than a conversation you only half understood.

Talk through the hour after school

Choose one ordinary part of the day and go through it in order. Who brings the child home? What happens before an activity starts? If the child wants a different game or a family member changes the plan, who decides what happens next?

Ask the parents to discuss these details with the grandparents too. You should be able to follow an agreed routine without carrying messages between adults. A picture-book session, for example, can have a clear start and finish, followed by a handover to a named family member.

Leave room for invitations and time on your own

Lunch with a grandparent or a weekend walk can be a pleasant way to get to know the family. Discuss whether an invitation is optional when it falls during your agreed time off. Explain that you may also want to meet friends, study or spend an afternoon alone.

Keep the activities you have agreed to do, your study plans and your time off in the same written schedule. If the family wants to add something, talk about it before it becomes part of the week.

Use one shared recap, then check how it is working

After matching, write a short recap together. For example: "One parent confirms changes to the weekly plan. We will discuss a different afternoon activity before changing it. Invitations during time off are optional." This is sample wording to adapt to the arrangements you actually agree on.

At the end of the first week, ask which parts of the day felt comfortable and which need changing. Include the grandparents in the conversation where it concerns a routine you share. Update the written plan so everyone can refer to the same version.

Bring the question into your search
