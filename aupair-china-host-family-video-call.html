Aupair China: Six Questions That Make a Host-Family Video Call Useful

A warm conversation is a beginning. These follow-up questions help both sides picture everyday life.

The video call ends with smiles. The parents seem kind, the child waves, and everyone says they are excited. You close your laptop and realise you still cannot picture a normal afternoon in their home. That is a useful discovery. A good first call does not have to answer everything, but it should give both sides a way to ask the next question.

1. Who will actually share the home and the decisions?

Ask who normally lives in the household, who is involved in childcare, and whom the au pair should speak to about everyday plans. A grandparent, another parent or a household helper may be important to the arrangement even if only one person joins the first call. The aim is to understand the household, not to demand that every relative appear on camera.

A useful follow-up is: 'If two adults give me different instructions, who should I check with?' Families can ask the mirror question: 'How would you raise it if an instruction felt unclear?' Clear communication matters more than a perfectly rehearsed introduction.

2. Can you describe yesterday, rather than an ideal day?

Ask a parent to describe a recent ordinary afternoon: when the child came home, what happened before dinner, which adult was present, and what the au pair would have been expected to do. Specific examples make words such as 'companionship', 'language exchange' and 'help' easier to understand.

If the family wants English practice, ask what that means in daily life. Reading together, playing a game and providing formal lessons are different expectations. The au pair should also describe the activities they are comfortable leading and where they would need guidance. Neither side benefits from agreeing to a role they are imagining differently.

3. What does private time and space look like?

Ask about the sleeping space, access to shared rooms, ordinary quiet hours and whether time outside agreed responsibilities is the au pair's own. If a room tour would help, agree a short tour with the adults first. Do not record the call or ask the family to reveal addresses, children's documents or other private details.

There is no need to judge the home by its decoration or size. The useful question is whether the arrangement offers a workable place to rest, study and close the door. A family can ask about habits that affect shared living too, such as late-night calls or food routines, without turning the conversation into a test of nationality.

4. How do you respond when someone gets something wrong?

Try: 'Could you give an example of how you would explain a household rule I had misunderstood?' A concrete answer can show how feedback is given: privately or in front of others, immediately or in a regular check-in, with space for questions or only instructions.

The au pair can offer a real example of receiving feedback and explain what helped them improve. Nobody needs to claim they never make mistakes. Look for a willingness to explain, listen and revisit an agreement. An imperfect English sentence or a nervous pause is not evidence of poor character or a bad match.

5. What happens when the plan changes or a concern remains?

Ask who handles an unexpected change and how it is discussed. Then ask a second question: 'If we have talked about a concern and it still is not resolved, what would we do next?' That takes the conversation beyond the reassuring phrase 'we are flexible'.

Write down the actual support contact and process for the arrangement you are considering. Do not assume every platform provides the same services, checks or emergency support. Both sides should understand what is included, who can help and what they will need to arrange independently.

6. Can we finish with a recap instead of an immediate yes?

A useful call ends with a next step: a short written recap, one or two unanswered questions and a time to speak again. The family and the au pair should both have room to correct the recap. Do not let a friendly conversation become pressure to commit before important details are clear.

You can send: 'Thank you for the call. I enjoyed meeting you. My understanding is that we discussed the daily role, household routines, private time and how concerns would be handled. I will send a brief recap so you can correct anything I misunderstood. Could we also clarify these two points before deciding?'

A simple way to read the answers

After the call, make three columns: clear enough, needs a follow-up, and does not fit my needs. A missing answer belongs in the follow-up column until you have more information. A requirement you cannot accept belongs in the third column even if everyone was pleasant. Host families can use the same exercise when reflecting on the applicant.
